## 1. Core idea

Large diffusion models and neural SDEs define complicated stochastic evolutions. The proposal is to represent such an evolution as a composition of several **complete stochastic processes**, where each component process runs over the full time horizon and has simpler, more interpretable mechanics.

This is not a division of the original trajectory into early, middle, and late intervals. Instead, it is a decomposition in **mechanistic depth**:

$$
\Phi_t
=
\Psi_t^{(K)}
\circ \cdots \circ
\Psi_t^{(1)}
\qquad
\text{for every } t.
$$

Here:

- $\Phi_t$ is the flow or pushforward induced by the original model at time $t$;
- each $\Psi_t^{(k)}$ is itself generated by a valid SDE or stochastic flow;
- each factor acts across the entire time domain;
- the factors should be easier to analyse than the original model;
- their composition should reproduce the original probability evolution.

The analogy is with deep linear models: a difficult global transformation may become understandable when decomposed into simpler interacting transformations.

## 2. Several possible formulations

These formulations are related, but they should not be treated as equivalent.

### A. Probability-path factorization

Factor the evolution of one chosen initial distribution:

$$
\mu_t
=
(\Psi_t^{(K)})_\#
\cdots
(\Psi_t^{(1)})_\#
\mu_0.
$$

Each factor transforms an entire probability path into another probability path.

This is the most flexible formulation and may be the easiest to learn. Its weakness is non-uniqueness: many different decompositions can reproduce the same marginals.

### B. Transition-operator factorization

Let $P_{s,t}$ be the Markov transition operator of the original process. Seek simpler Markov processes with operators $P_{s,t}^{(k)}$ such that

$$
P_{s,t}
=
P_{s,t}^{(1)}
P_{s,t}^{(2)}
\cdots
P_{s,t}^{(K)}.
$$

This is a stronger decomposition because it should work for arbitrary starting states or distributions, not only for the training distribution.

The main difficulty is that products of Markov evolutions do not automatically form a consistent Markov process unless the factors satisfy compatibility conditions.

### C. Pathwise stochastic-flow factorization

Under a coupling of the random noise, require

$$
\varphi_{s,t}
=
\psi_{s,t}^{(K)}
\circ \cdots \circ
\psi_{s,t}^{(1)}
$$

for almost every noise realization.

This preserves the random transformation itself, rather than only its probability distribution. It is the strongest and most difficult version.

### D. Factorization after a learned coordinate change

The original dynamics may not be simple in data coordinates. Learn an invertible representation $h$ such that

$$
h \circ \Phi_t \circ h^{-1}
$$

can be decomposed into simpler stochastic mechanisms.

This may be the most realistic nonlinear analogue of diagonalizing or separating modes in linear systems.

### E. Approximate mechanistic factorization

Exact equality may be unnecessarily restrictive. Instead, seek factors that reproduce the original probability flow within a measurable error tolerance while gaining substantial interpretability:

$$
\widehat{\Phi}_t
=
\Psi_t^{(K)}
\circ \cdots \circ
\Psi_t^{(1)}
\approx
\Phi_t.
$$

The main research question then becomes the trade-off between fidelity and simplicity.

## 3. What could make a component process “simple”?

A decomposition is useful only if simplicity is defined explicitly.

Possible notions include:

### Low-dimensional action

Each factor changes only a small subspace or learned distribution of directions.

Examples might include:

- global geometry;
- object identity;
- pose;
- texture;
- illumination;
- fine stochastic variation.

### Sparse or low-rank dynamics

The drift Jacobian or diffusion matrix of each factor could be sparse, block-structured, or low-rank.

### Triangular dependence

In suitable coordinates, factor $k$ depends only on a small subset of variables or on earlier factors in a directed hierarchy.

### Tractable distribution family

A factor may preserve or transform distributions within a family with closed-form sufficient statistics, such as Gaussian, affine, exponential-family, or mixture-based approximations.

### Simple observable dynamics

A small collection of observables could evolve according to a closed finite-dimensional system. This could make each mechanism analysable without solving the full SDE.

### Causal specialization

Intervening on one factor should alter a limited and stable set of final properties while leaving others approximately unchanged.

This is probably the strongest criterion for calling the factors mechanistic rather than merely mathematically convenient.

## 4. Why composition can remain expressive

If

$$
\Phi_t
=
\Psi_t^{(2)}
\circ
\Psi_t^{(1)},
$$

the velocity of the composite flow is not usually the simple sum of the two component velocities. The contribution of the first process is transformed by the second process.

Schematically,

$$
v_{\text{composite}}
=
v_2
+
(\Psi^{(2)})_* v_1.
$$

For several factors, earlier mechanisms are repeatedly transformed by later ones.

This means that individually simple processes can generate complicated global dynamics through interaction and conjugation. That is an important reason the decomposition might be expressive enough to model a large diffusion system.

## 5. Main theoretical problems

### Existence

When can a complex stochastic process be represented by a small number of simpler full-horizon processes?

Exact factorizations may require special structure such as:

- invariant subspaces or foliations;
- triangular dynamics;
- commuting or compatible generators;
- symmetries;
- separability after a coordinate transformation.

Approximate factorizations may exist much more broadly.

### Identifiability

Without constraints, one can insert an arbitrary invertible process and its inverse:

$$
\Phi_t
=
(\Phi_t \circ G_t^{-1}) \circ G_t.
$$

Therefore the decomposition is not automatically meaningful.

Possible ways to fix this ambiguity include:

- minimum description length;
- minimum number of factors;
- low-rank or sparse generators;
- minimal stochastic energy;
- independence or weak dependence between factors;
- orthogonality of causal effects;
- restrictions on the allowed coordinate transformations.

### Autonomy

The simplest interpretation would have each factor follow its own SDE independently of the others.

That may be too restrictive. More general possibilities include:

- autonomous factors;
- factors driven by shared noise;
- conditionally independent factors;
- hierarchical factors whose coefficients depend on earlier processes;
- coupled factors that remain individually analysable.

The degree of autonomy should be treated as an experimental question.

### Preservation criterion

The project must specify what is being preserved:

1. final distribution only;
2. marginal distribution at every time;
3. probability-flow ODE;
4. transition kernels;
5. complete stochastic path law.

Each level supports different claims about explanation.

## 6. Candidate research programs

### Program 1: Linear-Gaussian theory

Begin with

$$
dX_t
=
A(t)X_t\,dt
+
B(t)\,dW_t.
$$

Seek a product of simpler linear stochastic flows whose generators are diagonal, block-diagonal, sparse, low-rank, or normal.

Questions:

- When does an exact factorization exist?
- How unique is it?
- Can the factors be recovered from observations?
- How does factor interaction reconstruct the full generator?

This is the cleanest theoretical analogue of deep linear networks.

### Program 2: Learned stochastic coordinates

Train an invertible representation in which a pretrained diffusion process becomes approximately triangular or block-separable.

Each block would then define a simpler SDE operating over the full time horizon.

This formulation prioritizes discoverability over exactness.

### Program 3: Causal factor discovery

Learn several component processes jointly with intervention objectives.

A factor is considered meaningful when perturbing or replacing it produces a localized effect on generated samples.

This program emphasizes explanation and control rather than analytical solvability alone.

### Program 4: Operator-level decomposition

Factor the transition or probability-flow operators directly, possibly using restricted operator families.

This could produce stronger mathematical guarantees, although it may be computationally difficult.

### Program 5: Distribution-path decomposition

Introduce intermediate probability paths

$$
\mu_t^{(0)},\mu_t^{(1)},\ldots,\mu_t^{(K)}
$$

and require each factor to map one complete path into the next.

The paths themselves could be optimized to make every transition simple.

This is a flexible formulation that may connect naturally to optimal transport, stochastic interpolation, and flow matching.

## 7. A possible learning objective

Let

$$
\widehat{\Phi}_t
=
\Psi_t^{(K)}
\circ \cdots \circ
\Psi_t^{(1)}.
$$

A general objective could be

$$
\mathcal{L}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\text{fidelity}}
+
\lambda_{\text{simple}}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{simplicity}}
+
\lambda_{\text{causal}}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{causal}}
+
\lambda_{\text{ind}}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{independence}}.
$$

Possible terms:

- **Fidelity:** match teacher trajectories, vector fields, marginals, or transition kernels.
- **Simplicity:** penalize rank, Jacobian complexity, description length, or non-closure of selected observables.
- **Causal specialization:** encourage different factors to control distinct output properties.
- **Independence:** discourage redundant factors or overlapping stochastic effects.

The objective should not assume that one particular notion of simplicity will work. Comparing several simplicity biases may itself be a central contribution.

## 8. First experiments

### Experiment A: Linear systems

Generate linear SDEs with known hidden block or low-rank structure. Test whether the decomposition can recover it.

### Experiment B: Gaussian mixtures

Use analytically tractable scores and meaningful latent structure such as mode identity and within-mode variation.

Possible factors might separate:

- global transport;
- mode selection;
- within-mode refinement;
- residual stochastic variation.

### Experiment C: Small diffusion models

Distil a pretrained image diffusion model into two or three full-horizon stochastic factors.

Evaluate:

- probability-flow fidelity;
- sample quality;
- factor simplicity;
- stability across random seeds;
- intervention locality;
- whether factors transfer between models or datasets.

## 9. Relation to existing work

Several nearby areas already exist:

- decomposition of stochastic flows along known geometric distributions;
- skew-product and symmetry-based process decompositions;
- semigroup and transition-operator factorizations;
- stochastic interpolants and alternative SDE realizations of the same probability path;
- flow matching and probability-flow modelling;
- post-hoc studies of semantic phases in diffusion trajectories.

The likely novelty is not the abstract fact that stochastic flows can sometimes be decomposed. It is the combination of:

- starting from a trained neural SDE or diffusion model;
- learning the decomposition rather than prescribing it;
- using complete full-horizon stochastic factors;
- constraining each factor to have analysable pushforwards;
- preserving the original probability evolution;
- validating the factors through causal interventions.

A proper literature review should test each of these claims separately.

---

## 10. Central open question

The broad question is:

> Can a complicated learned stochastic evolution be represented as a small composition of complete stochastic processes whose individual pushforwards are simple enough to analyse and whose interactions explain the computation of the original model?

The project remains intentionally open about:

- the correct level of exactness;
- the best notion of simplicity;
- whether factors should be autonomous;
- whether decomposition should occur in data space or learned coordinates;
- whether the primary goal is theory, interpretation, compression, or controllability.

Those choices define several related research projects rather than a single fixed proposal.
